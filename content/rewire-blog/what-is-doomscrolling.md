You open your phone to check one headline. A related story leads to a comment thread, then another update. You are no longer finding much that is useful, but putting the phone down feels harder than refreshing it.

**Doomscrolling means repeatedly consuming upsetting or negative online content, even as it leaves you feeling worse.** That is the sense used in [Cleveland Clinic’s explanation of doomscrolling](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/everything-you-need-to-know-about-doomscrolling-and-how-to-avoid-it). The “doom” matters: spending longer than intended watching harmless videos may be unwanted scrolling, but negative content is central to this term.

We built [Rewire](/rewire/) to put a deliberate interruption in front of distracting apps and websites. It can be part of a plan to reduce doomscrolling. First, it helps to be clear about the behaviour you want to change.

# Doomscrolling or staying informed?

Reading difficult news can be purposeful. You might need an update about something affecting your family, work or community. A distressing subject does not make checking it a mistake.

Here is an illustrative distinction. You read a reliable report to answer “Has the advice changed?”, then close it. Compare that with repeatedly refreshing several feeds after you already have the answer, hoping the next update will finally feel reassuring.

Rather than giving yourself a label, ask:

- What did I open this to find out?
- Have I found it, or am I looking at the same information again?
- What would make this a good point to stop?

These are reflection prompts, not a diagnostic checklist or a research-based time limit.

# Why can it feel hard to stop?

Wanting more information when events feel uncertain is understandable. Cleveland Clinic’s psychologist describes how searching for updates can turn into an automatic habit. In research, [Satici and colleagues found associations between doomscrolling, fear of missing out and problematic social-media use](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11482-022-10110-7).

Those associations do not establish one explanation for everyone. Your own version might start with a notification, a difficult news event or a quiet moment before bed. Treat those as possibilities to notice, rather than a story an app can infer about you.

For example, you might write: “After dinner, I open the news app while waiting for the kettle.” That gives you a specific moment to work with. “I need to fix my relationship with my phone” is a much larger job.

# What does the research actually show?

There is evidence worth taking seriously, with limits worth keeping in view.

In [two experiments published in PLOS ONE in 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0257728), brief exposure to COVID-related content on Twitter or YouTube reduced positive mood compared with no exposure. Optimism also fell in the second study. These were immediate effects in a particular pandemic context; they do not show that every feed causes lasting harm.

The [doomscrolling-scale study published online in 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11482-022-10110-7) also found relationships with psychological distress and wellbeing. Its survey findings cannot establish that doomscrolling alone caused someone’s anxiety or low mood, or rule out influence in the other direction.

Neither study tested Rewire. Neither justifies promising that an app blocker will cure anxiety or save everyone a particular number of hours. The practical question is smaller: would a boundary help you stop checking when you intended to stop?

# Try one boundary you can actually use

[Cleveland Clinic recommends setting limits, putting the phone out of reach and paying attention to how the content affects you](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/everything-you-need-to-know-about-doomscrolling-and-how-to-avoid-it). Here is our suggested way to turn that advice into a small experiment. The timings are examples, not a proven dose.

## Decide what “caught up” means

Choose the question you want answered and where you will check it. You might decide to read one trusted summary after lunch rather than keep several live feeds open. If the situation requires timely safety information, leave the relevant alerts and services accessible.

## Protect a particular moment

Pick the part of the day you want back: dinner, a reading break or the half-hour before you planned to put your phone away. Start with a boundary you want to keep, then review whether it suited the day you actually had.

## Give the pause somewhere to lead

Decide on a simple next action: finish making tea, read the page in front of you or put the phone on its charger. It does not need to be a productive activity. Rest is a reasonable choice.

# What if a blocker is not enough?

If you keep finding ways around the boundary, use that as a reason to revisit the plan. Was the session too broad? Did you block something you actually needed? Is repeated checking tied to a worry that needs a conversation rather than another setting?

If distress or compulsive checking is interfering with daily life, consider speaking with a qualified mental-health professional. [Cleveland Clinic includes seeking professional help among its recommendations](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/everything-you-need-to-know-about-doomscrolling-and-how-to-avoid-it). An app can support a boundary; it is not mental-health treatment.
