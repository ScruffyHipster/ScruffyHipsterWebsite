# Three signs worth noticing

Clicking at the breast, a latch that will not stay put and sore nipples can leave you wondering whether tongue-tie is involved. It is an understandable question—and not one you need to answer alone.

A tongue-tie is when the small piece of skin under the tongue, called the frenulum, is shorter or tighter than usual. Some babies with a tongue-tie feed well and need no treatment. Others find it harder to move their tongue effectively, which can affect breastfeeding or bottle feeding.

The [NHS guidance on tongue-tie](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tongue-tie/) lists several possible signs. None is a diagnosis on its own; they are reasons to ask for a feeding assessment.

# 1. Repeated clicking, leaking or slipping off the breast

Clicking can happen when a baby loses suction during a feed. You may also notice that they repeatedly come off and relatch, dribble milk, seem windy, take a long time to feed or want to feed again soon afterwards.

Clicking alone does not mean tongue-tie. Positioning, attachment, milk flow and the ordinary learning curve of newborn feeding can all play a part. But if it comes with a shallow latch or difficulty staying attached, it is worth mentioning to your midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding supporter.

# 2. Feeding is painful, not just tender

Early tenderness can be common. Pain that continues through every feed, cracked or bleeding nipples, or nipples that look flattened, wedged or white after a feed need attention. Painful, overly full breasts can be a sign that feeding is not working comfortably either.

Tongue-tie is only one possible cause. The [NHS advice on sore or cracked nipples](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding-problems/sore-nipples/) recommends getting help early so someone can watch a feed, check attachment and look at other causes. You do not need to prove why it hurts before asking for help.

If your breast is hot, red or increasingly painful, or you feel feverish or unwell, get advice promptly. See the [NHS guidance on breast pain and breastfeeding](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding-problems/breast-pain/) for when to contact your GP or NHS 111.

# 3. Feeds feel long, unsettled or ineffective

Newborns often feed frequently, so frequency alone is not a warning sign. But it is sensible to ask for an assessment if your baby regularly feeds for a long time and still seems hungry, struggles to stay attached, or you are worried about weight gain or wet nappies.

Tongue-tie can make milk transfer harder for some babies, but there are many possible reasons for feeding difficulties. A feeding assessment can help you work out what needs attention and whether your baby needs extra milk while you make a plan.

If your baby is not feeding, is difficult to wake, or has not had a wet nappy or peed for 12 hours, follow the [NHS urgent advice for babies and children under 5](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/when-to-get-urgent-medical-help-for-babies-and-children-under-5/). Do not wait for a tongue-tie appointment if you are worried about your baby’s intake.

# Start with an assessment, not a verdict

A good assessment looks at how your baby’s tongue moves *and* how feeding is going. It should not be based only on the appearance of the frenulum.

Ask what the assessment found, what support could help, and what a division might realistically change. You can take time to think. You do not have to make a decision in the middle of a difficult feed or an emotional appointment.

# The feeding options are yours to choose

There is no single right response to a suspected tongue-tie. The best next step depends on your baby’s feeding and growth, your comfort, the support available and what feels manageable for your family.

## Tongue-tie division

Tongue-tie division, also called frenotomy or frenulum division, is a small procedure that cuts the piece of skin under the tongue. The NHS says it may be offered when tongue-tie is causing feeding problems.

For some families, this is the right choice after a specialist assessment. For others, it is not recommended, is not available locally or does not feel right. It may improve feeding, but it does not guarantee an immediate change: attachment and feeding support can still matter afterwards. Your clinician should explain the likely benefits, risks and aftercare for your baby.

## Continue breastfeeding with support

You may choose to keep breastfeeding without a procedure. Some babies feed more comfortably with time and support around positioning and attachment. That does not mean enduring pain alone: it can include a plan with your infant-feeding team, monitoring weight and nappies, or expressing milk for some feeds.

You can review this choice later. Waiting does not close off every other option.

## Combi feeding

Combi feeding can mean breastfeeding alongside bottles of expressed milk, formula, or both. It can give your baby another way to receive milk while keeping breastfeeding in the picture, if that is what you want.

The [NHS guidance on combining breast and bottle feeding](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/combine-breast-and-bottle/) explains that formula can affect milk supply. If you want to replace breastfeeds with formula, reducing feeds gradually can help lower the chance of engorgement and mastitis.

## Stop breastfeeding and use formula

You may decide breastfeeding is no longer right for you, whether or not your baby has a tongue-tie. That is a valid decision. If your baby is under 1, dropped breastfeeds need to be replaced with first infant formula.

The [NHS advice on stopping breastfeeding](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/how-to-stop/) recommends doing this gradually where possible, to give you both time to adjust and reduce the risk of engorgement and mastitis.

# The goal is a feeding plan that works

You might choose a division and still use formula. You might breastfeed without a procedure, introduce bottles later or move fully to formula. Feeding can change as you learn what works.

These are not moral choices. Your baby needs to be fed, and you need a plan that protects your physical and emotional wellbeing. If you are in pain or worried about milk transfer, ask for a feeding assessment from your midwife, health visitor, GP or local infant-feeding team.

# Where to get help

The NHS lists the National Breastfeeding Helpline on **0300 100 0212**. You can also speak to your midwife, health visitor, GP or local infant-feeding team.

This article is general information, not a diagnosis or substitute for medical advice. Check the current NHS guidance and seek support for your own situation.
