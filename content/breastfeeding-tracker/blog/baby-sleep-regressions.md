# Just when you thought you had found a rhythm

Perhaps bedtime had started to feel familiar. You could roughly guess when the next feed would happen, or count on a stretch of sleep before hearing that little voice again.

Then something changed. Your baby began waking more often, naps became shorter, or settling suddenly took much longer. At two in the morning, it is easy to wonder what you have done differently.

**A change in your baby’s sleep does not automatically mean you have done something wrong.**

“Sleep regression” is a common name for a period when sleep becomes more unsettled after feeling relatively predictable. It describes the change; it does not explain every cause. Understanding that distinction can take some pressure off while helping you notice when your baby needs more support.

# What does a sleep regression look like?

You might notice more waking overnight, shorter naps, extra fussiness around sleep or a baby who needs more help settling than before. Your baby does not have to have slept through the night for a change to feel significant.

These patterns are often called a regression, but they can also overlap with hunger, discomfort or illness. [Cleveland Clinic’s explanation of infant sleep regressions](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/the-4-month-sleep-regression-what-parents-need-to-know) makes the same practical point: do not assume every difficult night has the same cause.

Start with your baby as a whole. Are they feeding as usual? Do they seem comfortable and alert when awake? Have their wet nappies changed? The answers matter more than whether a sleep chart says a regression is due.

# Why does everyone talk about the four-month regression?

Sleep develops during infancy. The [American Academy of Pediatrics explains that regular sleep cycles begin to emerge around four months](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/getting-your-baby-to-sleep.aspx). That helps explain why this age comes up so often in conversations about changing sleep.

It does not mean every baby will suddenly start waking on the same week, or that all waking at this age comes from one developmental change.

[BASIS, the Baby Sleep Information Source](https://www.basisonline.org.uk/infant-sleep-biology/), describes sleep development as individual and uneven. Babies who have begun sleeping for longer stretches may wake more again later. Calling this a regression can make ordinary development sound like a step backwards, when sleep is still taking shape.

You have not necessarily lost a skill you taught your baby. And a baby who never had long stretches of sleep has not missed a milestone.

# Do regressions happen at set ages?

You may see lists of four-, eight-, twelve- or eighteen-month regressions. They can offer a reassuring label, but they are not a calendar every child follows. Cleveland Clinic notes that research has not established a fixed schedule of regressions for all babies.

Other changes can affect nights too. The [NHS guidance on baby sleep](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/) names growth spurts, teething and illness as possible influences.

Later in infancy, some babies also become more upset when a familiar adult leaves. [Separation anxiety is common from around six months to three years](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/behaviour/separation-anxiety/). If settling becomes harder when you step away, that may be part of the picture, rather than a new problem you have created.

# How long does a sleep regression last?

There is no reliable countdown for your own baby. Cleveland Clinic describes many episodes as lasting around a week or two, but that is a general guide, not a deadline or a reason to dismiss ongoing difficulties.

If nights remain hard, it is more useful to review feeding, comfort, routines and family wellbeing than to keep waiting for a particular week to end.

You can ask your health visitor for support at any point. You do not need to complete a set number of sleepless nights first.

# What can help during an unsettled patch?

## Keep bedtime simple and familiar

A short wind-down can give the evening a recognisable shape: a nappy change, sleep clothes, a quiet song or story and a cuddle. It does not need to be elaborate or happen at the exact same minute.

The NHS suggests keeping lights low and interaction quiet during night feeds and changes. Daytime can include normal light, conversation and play. These are gentle cues, not a promise of uninterrupted sleep.

## Respond to the baby in front of you

Check whether your baby needs feeding, changing or comfort before deciding they should be asleep. If they are upset, it is reasonable to help them settle.

BASIS describes physical contact as soothing for many babies and cautions that rigid sleep timings can become frustrating as needs change. A routine should leave room for the day you are actually having.

If your current way of settling has become difficult to sustain, tell your health visitor what is happening and what you would like to change. You can ask for a plan that fits your baby’s age and your family’s preferences.

## Make the support practical

“Let me know if you need anything” can be hard to answer when you are exhausted. A specific request may be easier: someone to bring dinner, handle an older child’s bedtime or care for the baby while you rest.

The [Lullaby Trust’s advice on sleep deprivation](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/being-a-parent-or-caregiver/sleep-deprivation/) encourages sharing care where possible. Support does not have to mean sharing every feed. Another adult can help with changing, settling or the jobs that would otherwise fill your chance to sleep.

# Does more waking mean my baby needs different milk or solids?

Waking alone cannot tell you whether your baby is getting enough milk. Keep responding to feeding cues, and ask for a feeding assessment if you are concerned about intake, nappies or growth.

Night feeds can still be needed. A regression label is not a reason to stretch feeds or remove them; decisions about reducing night feeds should take your baby’s age, health and growth into account with professional support.

Introducing solids is not a sleep treatment. The [NHS advice on first solid foods](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/weaning-and-feeding/babys-first-solid-foods/) says extra waking and wanting more milk are not, on their own, signs of readiness. Solids are generally introduced around six months when the readiness signs are present, and starting them will not make a baby more likely to sleep through.

Whether you breastfeed, bottle feed or combine the two, you deserve help with difficult nights. Feeding choices do not need to become a verdict on how well you are parenting.

# Keep safer sleep the same, even when nights change

Tiredness can make a product or position that promises longer sleep sound especially appealing. Keep the basics consistent for every sleep, including naps.

The [Lullaby Trust’s safer sleep guidance](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/safer-sleep-information/safer-sleep-overview/) recommends:

- placing your baby on their back;
- using their own clear sleep space with a firm, flat mattress;
- keeping pillows, toys, cot bumpers and loose items out;
- having your baby sleep in the same room as you for at least the first six months;
- avoiding overheating and keeping your baby smoke-free.

If you swaddle, [stop as soon as your baby shows signs of trying to roll](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/baby-product-information/swaddling/).

**Never fall asleep with your baby on a sofa or armchair.** If you feel yourself nodding off, put your baby back in their cot or ask an awake adult to take over. Read the Lullaby Trust’s advice on tiredness before another difficult night, rather than relying on willpower to stay awake.

# When to ask for help

Speak to your health visitor or GP if sleep changes persist, feeding has become difficult, weight gain concerns you, or exhaustion is making everyday life hard. Your wellbeing is part of the picture too.

Do not put signs of illness down to a regression. Get prompt advice if your baby is taking less milk, has fewer wet nappies, seems in pain or is unusually sleepy. If you are unsure what to do, call NHS 111.

Follow the [NHS urgent-care guidance for babies and children under five](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/when-to-get-urgent-medical-help-for-babies-and-children-under-5/) immediately if your baby seems seriously unwell. **Call 999 or go to A&E** for difficulty breathing, blue or grey skin or lips, a baby who is difficult to wake, or a seizure. The NHS also lists a temperature of **38°C or above in a baby under three months**, and **no urine for twelve hours**, as reasons for immediate help.

You do not need to be certain what is wrong before asking someone to assess your baby.

# You have not gone back to the beginning

Knowing that sleep can change does not make a broken night less tiring. It can, however, make it feel less like a test you are failing.

For now, a useful aim is a baby whose needs are being met, a safe place to sleep and enough support to get you through the next day. You can adjust the routine as you go. You do not have to solve every future night tonight.
