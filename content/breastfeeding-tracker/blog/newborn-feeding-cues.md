# Is that another feeding cue?

You have barely put the muslin down, and your newborn is moving their mouth again. Their hand goes up to their face. You glance at the time and wonder whether they can already want another feed.

**You can offer a feed when your baby shows early hunger cues, before they start crying.** The [NHS guide to the first days of breastfeeding](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/the-first-few-days/) describes responding to your baby's signals rather than waiting for a set time.

Getting to know those signals takes practice. Missing a quiet cue does not mean you have let your baby down. There will be another chance to notice, respond and ask for help if something feels difficult.

# What do early feeding cues look like?

Your baby may begin looking for something to suck, bring their hands towards their mouth, open and close their mouth, or become more wriggly. Rooting is the searching movement they make as they look for the breast or bottle teat. The [NHS lists these among the signs of hunger](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding-your-baby/feeding-on-demand/).

You do not need to wait for every sign to appear. Try watching the small changes as your baby becomes ready for a feed, rather than turning the list into a score they have to reach.

This is a way to become more familiar with your own baby. It is not a test you should already know how to pass.

# Do you have to wait for crying?

Crying is a later hunger signal. The NHS explains that spotting earlier cues can make feeding easier. If your baby is already upset, a cuddle or skin-to-skin contact may help them settle before you try feeding. See its [responsive-feeding guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding-your-baby/feeding-on-demand/).

That advice is not a reason to blame yourself for a crying baby. Sometimes the first sign you notice will be a loud one, particularly when you are also caring for another child or trying to get dressed.

Babies also cry for reasons other than hunger. The [NHS advice on soothing a crying baby](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/soothing-a-crying-baby/) includes tiredness, a wet or dirty nappy and wanting a cuddle. If crying is persistent, unusual or accompanied by signs of illness, ask for medical advice rather than trying to decode it alone.

# What if the last feed was only a little while ago?

Early feeding can feel much less regular than the notes you made before your baby arrived. The [NHS describes frequent feeding in the first weeks](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/the-first-few-days/), including periods when a baby wants to feed very often.

The time of the last feed is useful background. It does not decide whether you should respond to the baby in front of you. A record can tell you what you wrote down; it cannot tell you whether your baby has had enough milk.

If your baby was premature, is unwell, has jaundice or weight concerns, or you have been given a feeding plan, follow the advice from your maternity or neonatal team. Ask them what to do if your baby is sleepy or shows few cues. This general guide should not replace an individual plan or encourage you to wait indefinitely for a signal.

# Can you use the same approach with a bottle?

Responsive feeding applies to bottles too. The NHS recommends following hunger cues and letting your baby decide when they have had enough. **Do not make your baby finish a bottle.** Its [bottle-feeding guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding-your-baby/feeding-on-demand/) explains how to give pauses and respond during the feed.

If you use breastfeeding, expressed milk and formula at different times, you do not need a different standard of attentiveness for each. Tell the person taking over what you have noticed, and give them space to watch your baby too.

# A handover can start with what you see

When someone else joins you, “They only just fed” can accidentally become the whole conversation. Our practical suggestion is to share an observation before discussing the log.

For example: “They've started turning and opening their mouth. I recorded the last bottle, but let's look at what they're showing us now.” This is an illustrative handover, not a rule about when to give the next feed.

The other adult might bring what you need, sit with you or take over a household job while you feed. You can agree one useful task without having to explain the whole day.

# When should you ask for help?

If you are worried about milk intake, painful feeding, nappies or growth, speak to your midwife, health visitor or feeding specialist. You can ask someone to watch a feed. The [NHS guidance on whether a breastfed baby is getting enough milk](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding-problems/enough-milk/) considers feeding, wet and dirty nappies and weight together, and recommends seeking help early.

You do not need a complete log before making that call. “I'm finding the cues difficult to read, and I'm worried about feeding” is enough to start.

For a baby who is unwell or not feeding, get prompt medical advice. In the UK, call **NHS 111** if you are worried or unsure what to do. **Call 999 or go to A&E** for difficulty breathing, blue or grey skin or lips, difficulty waking, or a seizure. The [NHS urgent-care guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/when-to-get-urgent-medical-help-for-babies-and-children-under-5/) also lists a temperature of **38°C or above in a baby under three months**, and **no urine for twelve hours**, as reasons for immediate help.

For an ordinary feeding moment, the next step can be small: notice the movement, offer a feed and ask for support when you need it.
