# The short answer

If you are choosing a standard first infant formula for a healthy baby, you do not need to buy the most expensive tin on the shelf.

The [NHS guidance on choosing formula](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/bottle-feeding/how-to-make-up-a-feed/how-to-use-formula/) is unusually direct: every infant formula sold in the UK **must** meet the same standards, and every brand will meet a baby's nutritional needs regardless of price.

That means a lower-priced first infant formula, such as Aldi's Mamia Stage 1 First Infant Milk, is not nutritionally inferior simply because it costs less. A premium-looking tin does not buy a baby a higher standard of required nutrition.

Brands are not necessarily identical. They may use different oils, protein sources or optional ingredients, and they can differ in price, packaging, availability and how easily an individual baby accepts them. But all standard first infant formulas legally sold in the UK have to satisfy the rules intended to make them suitable for a healthy baby's growth and development.

# Why all first infant formula has to meet strict standards

Infant formula is not regulated like an ordinary grocery product.

The UK has detailed rules covering its composition, labelling and sale. Government [nutrition legislation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/nutrition-legislation-information-sources/nutrition-legislation-information-sheet--2) explains that these rules set compositional requirements informed by scientific evidence. They also restrict nutrition and health claims on infant formula.

There is good reason for that level of control. First infant formula can be a baby's main source of nutrition during the early months, so manufacturers cannot simply decide for themselves what essential nutrients to include or use branding as a substitute for nutritional adequacy.

In its UK infant formula market study, the Competition and Markets Authority said parents need clearer information that [all infant formula products meet the nutritional and safety needs of healthy babies, and that cheaper products are not nutritionally inferior](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/infant-formula-and-follow-on-formula-market-study-final-report/overview).

That is the important distinction: the law sets the nutritional floor every product must clear. A higher retail price is not evidence that one standard first infant formula nourishes a healthy baby better than another.

# So, is Aldi Mamia as good as a more expensive formula?

For meeting the regulated nutritional needs of a healthy baby, Aldi Mamia First Infant Milk has to meet the same UK requirements just like more expensive first infant formula brands.

As of 14 August 2026, Aldi lists [Mamia Stage 1 First Infant Milk](https://www.aldi.co.uk/brands/mamia) at £6.99 for 800g. *Prices and availability can change*, but the underlying point does not: being an own-brand or lower-priced product does not exempt a formula from the legal standards.

The Competition and Markets Authority reached a similar practical conclusion in 2025. It found that parents could save around £300 a year by moving to a lower-priced brand, while also stressing that all infant formula meets babies' full nutritional needs. Its concern was that branding can lead parents to interpret a higher price as higher quality when the required nutrition is already tightly controlled.

So it is reasonable to choose Mamia—or another standard first infant formula—because it is affordable and available to you. You are not short-changing your baby by refusing to pay a premium for branding.

# The formulas meet the same requirements, but they are not identical

“They all meet the same standards” is not the same as saying every tin contains precisely the same recipe.

Within the rules, manufacturers can use different formulations and add permitted optional ingredients. Packaging, scoop design, smell and taste may differ too. One brand may be easier to find locally, available in a ready-to-feed format or better suited to your family's practical needs.

Those differences can matter to a parent. They do not prove that a more expensive standard formula provides better required nutrition.

When choosing, it may be more useful to ask:

- Is it labelled **first infant formula**, **first milk** or **stage 1**, and suitable from birth?
- Can we reliably buy it nearby?
- Is the ongoing cost manageable for our family?
- Does the format work for us at home and when travelling?
- Does our baby accept it?

There is no prize for choosing the most expensive answer to those questions.

# First infant formula is different from specialist formula

This comparison is about standard first infant formula for healthy babies. It does not apply in the same way to a specialist formula recommended for a medical or feeding need.

Anti-reflux, lactose-free, extensively hydrolysed and amino-acid formulas serve different purposes. Some should only be used with advice from a health professional. If your baby has been prescribed or recommended a particular formula, or you are worried about feeding, growth, allergies, reflux or another symptom, speak to your midwife, health visitor, GP or paediatric team before changing it.

The cheapest standard tin is not an alternative to medical advice. But neither should the price of a standard brand be mistaken for medical superiority.

# You do not need to move to follow-on formula at six months

The NHS advises that first infant formula can remain a baby's main drink until 12 months old. There is no need to switch to follow-on formula at six months, and research has not shown a benefit from doing so.

That can make shopping simpler: look for first infant formula rather than treating each numbered stage as a necessary upgrade.

Formula should always be prepared exactly as instructed on the packaging. The NHS recommends making feeds one at a time, using freshly boiled drinking water that has cooled for no more than 30 minutes so it remains at least 70°C, and never adding extra powder or extra water. See the current [NHS guidance on making up baby formula](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/making-up-baby-formula/) for the full method.

# Choosing on price is a responsible choice

Parents are often making formula decisions when they are tired, worried and keen to do everything possible for their baby. In that moment, a higher price can feel reassuring. It can quietly suggest that spending more means caring more.

The evidence does not support that guilt.

For a healthy baby using standard first infant formula, every UK product must meet strict nutritional requirements. An affordable formula your baby accepts and your family can reliably buy is a sensible choice. If that is Aldi Mamia, you do not need to apologise for it or wonder whether the expensive tin would have loved your baby better.

Price is not nutrition. Branding is not parenting. Feeding your baby safely, responsively and in a way your family can sustain is what matters.
