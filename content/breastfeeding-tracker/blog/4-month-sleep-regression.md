# When the nights change again

Perhaps you had stopped counting every wake-up. There was a stretch of sleep you could roughly rely on, and feeding in the dark had become familiar. Then, somewhere around four months, the nights changed.

If that sounds like your house, you do not need another perfect routine to fail at. You need a clearer idea of what might be happening, what deserves attention and what can wait until morning.

**The four-month sleep regression is a common description of more unsettled sleep, not a deadline every baby meets.** Sleep development varies, and a difficult night cannot tell you its cause. [Cleveland Clinic’s explanation of infant sleep regressions](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/the-4-month-sleep-regression-what-parents-need-to-know) describes that variation and cautions against assuming every wake-up is a regression.

# What changes around four months?

The [American Academy of Pediatrics explains that regular sleep cycles emerge around four months](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/getting-your-baby-to-sleep.aspx). That is a useful developmental landmark, rather than a promise that nights become predictable at that age.

[BASIS, the Baby Sleep Information Source](https://www.basisonline.org.uk/infant-sleep-biology/), describes development as uneven: longer stretches may be followed by more waking. Your baby has not necessarily lost a skill, and a baby who has never slept for long stretches has not failed a milestone.

For the wider picture at other ages, read our [guide to baby sleep regressions](/breastfeeding-tracker/blog/baby-sleep-regressions/). Here, we are focusing on the feeding questions and practical decisions that can make four months feel particularly confusing.

# What might you notice, and how long will it last?

More waking, shorter naps and longer settling are commonly described signs. Cleveland Clinic gives a week or two as a typical unsettled spell, but also stresses that timing varies. **That estimate is not a countdown for your baby**, or a reason to postpone help.

Try describing the change in ordinary language: “The first stretch is shorter than it was,” or “Naps are harder, but feeding seems the same.” That gives you something concrete to discuss without having to decide whether the label fits.

# Does more waking mean breastfeeding is not working?

More waking by itself is not a feeding assessment. The [NHS notes that some babies aged three to six months sleep for longer stretches, but others do not](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/). Four months is not an automatic cut-off for night feeds.

Keep feeding responsively rather than using a regression label to stretch the gaps. If feeding has changed or you are worried about intake, ask your health visitor or feeding specialist to assess it with you. [NHS infant-feeding guidance](https://derbyshirefamilyhealthservice.nhs.uk/breastfeeding) supports responding to feeding cues and seeking help with concerns.

You can say: “My baby is waking more and I am worried about milk intake. Can we talk through feeds and growth?” You do not need a complete spreadsheet before making that call.

Nor is extra waking a reason on its own to start solids. The [NHS recommends introducing solids around six months, when readiness signs are present](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/weaning-and-feeding/babys-first-solid-foods/), and says starting solids does not make sleeping through more likely.

# A manageable plan for tonight

Keep the plan small enough to use when tired. The [NHS baby-sleep guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/helping-your-baby-to-sleep/) suggests a simple wind-down and quiet, dimly lit nights. It also recommends asking for help with disrupted sleep.

Our practical suggestion is to agree three things before bedtime:

1. **A familiar wind-down.** Choose a short sequence you can manage, such as a change, feed, cuddle and quiet song. Treat it as a routine, not a test of independent settling.
2. **One useful job to share.** If someone can help, be specific: changing, resettling after a feed or taking the morning household jobs.
3. **A plan for your own tiredness.** Decide where the baby can safely go if you start nodding off, and who you can ask to take over.

The [Lullaby Trust’s sleep-deprivation advice](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/being-a-parent-or-caregiver/sleep-deprivation/) is clear about planning ahead. Never sleep with your baby on a sofa or armchair. If you feel yourself drifting off, put your baby in their safe sleep space or ask an awake adult to take over.

# What is worth tracking?

If a record would help you explain the change, try a few days of simple notes. This is an optional memory aid, not a prescribed monitoring routine. We would start with:

- roughly when sleep started and ended, including naps;
- wake-ups you noticed, without trying to capture every movement;
- feeds and any concern you want to discuss;
- a brief note about settling or what felt different that day.

For example: “Asleep about 8.15; woke around 10.40; fed; settled again about 11.10.” That is an illustrative entry, not a recommended bedtime or feeding interval.

Approximate times are fine. Look back when you have the headspace, rather than watching a timer all night. If recording becomes another source of pressure, simplify it or stop. The record is there to support a conversation, not score your baby’s sleep.

# Keep safer sleep consistent

For every sleep, place your baby on their back in a clear cot with a firm, flat mattress, in your room for at least the first six months. Avoid overheating, smoke exposure, pillows, bumpers and soft toys. See the [Lullaby Trust’s safer-sleep guidance](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/safer-sleep-information/safer-sleep-overview/).

If you swaddle, [stop when your baby shows signs of trying to roll](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/baby-product-information/swaddling/). Do not wait for a particular age or a completed roll.

# When to ask for help

Speak to your health visitor or GP if feeding worries you, nights remain difficult or exhaustion is becoming hard to manage. You do not have to wait for a regression to “finish”.

Do not explain illness away as sleep development. In the UK, call **NHS 111** if you are worried or unsure. **Call 999 or go to A&E** for breathing difficulty, blue or grey skin or lips, difficulty waking or a seizure. Follow the [full NHS urgent-care guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/when-to-get-urgent-medical-help-for-babies-and-children-under-5/), including its age-specific fever advice.

For tonight, a worthwhile aim is modest: respond to your baby, keep sleep safe and make room for some support for you. You do not have to solve the next six months before going back to bed.
