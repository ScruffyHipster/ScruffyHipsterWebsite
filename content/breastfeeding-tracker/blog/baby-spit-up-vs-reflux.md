# The short answer

Spit-up and reflux are not really two separate things. **Spit-up is usually what you see when ordinary reflux brings a little milk back up into your baby’s mouth.**

For many babies, it happens without effort or distress. They bring up milk during or soon after a feed, then carry on looking comfortable. If your baby is otherwise well and gaining weight, this common kind of reflux usually improves as they grow.

The more useful question is not simply “Is this spit-up or reflux?” It is:

**Is my baby comfortable, feeding and growing—or are there signs that this needs medical attention?**

This guide explains what to notice without asking you to diagnose your baby from a muslin cloth.

# What do spit-up, reflux and GORD mean?

Reflux is the movement of stomach contents back up into the food pipe. When milk reaches the mouth and dribbles out, people commonly call it spit-up, possetting or regurgitation.

The [NHS guidance on reflux in babies](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/reflux-in-babies/) says reflux is very common, usually begins before 8 weeks and often gets better by the time a baby is 1. It happens partly because the ring of muscle that helps keep milk in the stomach is still developing.

You may also hear several medical abbreviations:

- **GOR** or **GER** means gastro-oesophageal or gastroesophageal reflux: milk moving back up.
- **GORD** or **GERD** means gastro-oesophageal reflux disease: reflux associated with troublesome symptoms or complications that need clinical management.

The UK usually uses GOR and GORD, while US sources use GER and GERD. The underlying distinction is the same: reflux itself is common; reflux disease is more than an ordinary milk dribble.

# What ordinary baby spit-up can look like

Normal spit-up is usually effortless. It may appear with a burp, run gently from the mouth or show up on your shoulder after a feed. Fresh milk can look thin, while milk that has spent longer in the stomach may look curdled. Appearance alone does not tell you how serious it is.

A baby with uncomplicated reflux is often described as a “happy spitter”. The phrase is informal, but the pattern is useful:

- your baby is generally comfortable before and after bringing up milk;
- feeds are going reasonably well;
- your baby has the expected wet nappies for their age;
- growth is following the pattern expected by their healthcare professional;
- the milk comes up without repeated forceful vomiting;
- your baby otherwise seems alert and well.

The wet patch can make a small amount of milk look surprisingly dramatic. Trying to judge the exact volume from clothing or a muslin is rarely reliable. Your baby’s comfort, feeding, nappies and growth tell you more than the apparent size of the patch.

# How is vomiting different from spit-up?

Spit-up tends to flow out with little effort. Vomiting involves a more forceful contraction and may travel away from the mouth rather than simply dribbling down the chin.

One forceful episode does not give you a diagnosis, but repeated or increasingly forceful vomiting needs medical advice—especially in a young baby. The colour matters too. Green or yellow-green vomit can be a sign of a blockage, and blood needs assessment.

If you are unsure whether what you saw was spit-up or vomiting, note what happened before and afterwards. Was it forceful? What colour was it? Did your baby seem distressed, unusually sleepy or keen to feed again? A photograph of the vomit and a brief record can be useful when speaking to a health professional, but do not delay urgent help to collect evidence.

# When reflux may need an assessment

Frequent milk coming back up does not automatically mean GORD. What matters is the wider pattern.

Speak to your health visitor, GP or another qualified healthcare professional if reflux seems to be causing discomfort, feeding is difficult, or you are worried. Reasons to ask for an assessment include:

- refusing feeds or repeatedly struggling to feed;
- marked distress during or after feeds;
- coughing, gagging or choking that keeps happening around feeds;
- not gaining weight as expected, or losing weight;
- fewer wet nappies or another concern about milk intake;
- reflux that is not improving despite simple feeding support;
- reflux beginning for the first time after 6 months;
- frequent reflux continuing beyond 1 year.

Symptoms such as crying, back arching or unsettled feeding can have more than one cause. Reflux, feeding difficulties and cow’s milk allergy can overlap, and ordinary newborn crying can be mistaken for reflux disease. A proper assessment should look at the whole baby rather than one symptom in isolation.

# Get urgent help for these signs

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or call **NHS 111** if your baby:

- has green or yellow vomit, or blood in their vomit;
- is projectile vomiting;
- has blood in their poo;
- has a swollen or tender tummy;
- keeps being sick and cannot keep fluid down;
- has signs of dehydration;
- refuses to feed;
- will not stop crying and is very distressed;
- has a very high temperature or feels unusually hot, cold or shivery.

Call **999 or go to A&E** if your baby is having difficulty breathing, has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin or lips, is difficult to wake, or you think something is seriously wrong. The NHS advises trusting your instincts when a baby seems seriously unwell.

These signs are not a checklist for GORD. Some can point to other conditions that need prompt assessment.

# What you can try when your baby is otherwise well

If your baby is happy, healthy and gaining weight, ordinary reflux usually does not need treatment. A few practical changes may make feeds and clean-up easier.

## Ask someone to watch a feed

A midwife, health visitor or trained feeding supporter can look at positioning, attachment and how your baby manages the flow of milk. This can help whether you are breastfeeding, bottle feeding or doing both.

Fast milk flow, a shallow attachment, swallowing air or a bottle teat that does not suit your baby can all affect how a feed feels. That does not mean you caused the reflux. It simply gives you some practical things to explore with support.

## Keep your baby upright while awake

The NHS suggests holding your baby upright during feeding and for as long as possible afterwards, and burping them regularly during feeds. Follow your baby’s cues rather than repeatedly interrupting a comfortable feed just to produce a burp.

## Keep sleep flat, clear and on the back

Even if your baby has reflux, put them to sleep flat on their back. **Do not raise the head of the cot or Moses basket, and do not place your baby on their side or front to sleep.** Upright holding is for when your baby is awake and supervised; it does not replace safe sleep guidance.

## Get advice before changing milk or using treatments

Do not start thickening feeds, removing foods from a breastfeeding parent’s diet, changing to a specialist formula or giving reflux medicines without individual advice.

For some babies with troublesome symptoms, a clinician may recommend a feeding assessment, a carefully managed formula change, a thickener or medicine. Those choices depend on the baby and are not routine treatment for ordinary spit-up. NICE advises against acid-suppressing medicines when visible regurgitation is the only symptom.

# A simple record can help—without measuring every stain

The [NHS bottle-feeding guidance on reflux](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding-challenges/reflux-and-bottle-feeding/) suggests keeping a record of feeds, how often milk comes back up and when your baby seems distressed. This can help a health visitor or GP understand the pattern.

Keep it light. You might note:

- when the feed happened and whether it was breast milk, formula or both;
- whether the milk dribbled up or was forceful;
- its colour;
- whether your baby seemed comfortable or distressed;
- wet nappies and any advice you were given.

You do not need to estimate every millilitre or log every laundry change. A record is there to support a conversation, not to diagnose reflux or turn feeding into a test.

# The thing to remember

A baby can spit up often and still be well. Another baby can bring up less milk but show feeding difficulty, pain or poor growth that deserves attention.

So look beyond the stain. Ordinary spit-up is usually visible reflux from a digestive system that is still developing. Comfort, feeding, nappies, growth and the presence—or absence—of red flags are more useful than how much milk the muslin appears to hold.

If you are uneasy, ask. You do not have to wait until a symptom looks dramatic before speaking to your health visitor or GP.

# FAQs

## Is spit-up the same as reflux?

Spit-up is usually the visible result of reflux: milk moves back up from the stomach and comes out of the mouth. Reflux can also happen without visible spit-up if the milk is swallowed again.

## How do I know whether my baby has reflux disease?

Frequency alone cannot diagnose GORD or GERD. Reflux disease is considered when reflux is associated with troublesome symptoms or complications such as marked distress, feeding difficulty or faltering growth. A healthcare professional should assess the whole pattern.

## Is curdled baby spit-up normal?

Milk can look curdled after mixing with stomach contents. That appearance on its own does not show whether reflux is serious. Seek advice based on your baby’s wellbeing and feeding, and get urgent help for green, yellow or bloody vomit, projectile vomiting or other red flags.

## Can a baby with reflux sleep on their side or with the cot raised?

No. NHS and NICE guidance says babies should sleep flat on their back. Do not raise the head of the cot or Moses basket, even when a baby has reflux.
