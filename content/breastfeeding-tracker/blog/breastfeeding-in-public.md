# Plan a place to stop

Breastfeeding in public can mean using an unfamiliar chair, moving your clothes and finding somewhere for the changing bag, all while holding your baby. For a first outing, choose one place where you can sit down and take your time over a feed.

A nearby cafe you know, a library with seating or a local breastfeeding group could be a starting point. Check opening times and think about where you would sit. The [NHS suggests planning a comfortable place to feed](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/breastfeeding-in-public/) and asking friends, your midwife or health visitor for local recommendations.

You might meet someone for a drink and leave the shopping for another day. If a friend or partner comes along, ask them to carry the bag, order or find a seat while you settle your baby. Agree that there is time to stop for a feed, including another one before you head home.

# What should I wear?

Try feeding in the clothes you plan to wear before you go out. Sit down, move the top and bra as you would for a feed, and check whether you can hold your baby comfortably. A zip or button that is awkward at home will still need managing in a cafe.

A loose top that lifts up may be enough. If you prefer to keep your tummy covered, you could wear a stretchy vest underneath and pull it down while lifting the outer layer. These are [clothing options described by the NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/breastfeeding/breastfeeding-in-public/), not clothes you have to buy.

**You do not need to cover up to feed.** If you choose clothing for more coverage, keep fabric away from your baby's face so you can see them clearly. A quieter seat is another option if you want more privacy.

# What is worth taking?

Pack your usual changing supplies and put anything you expect to use within reach before the feed starts. For example:

- **A drink and snack for you.** A water bottle can stay beside your chair.
- **A spare top or breast pads, if you use them.** Choose these according to what you already find useful at home.
- **Your usual cloth for spills.** Keep it for wiping up, with your baby's face uncovered during the feed.

You do not need a separate set of nursing equipment for an outing. If you rely on cushions or a particular position at home, ask your midwife or breastfeeding supporter to help you try a seated position you could use elsewhere.

# When your baby wants to feed

Look for the feeding cues you recognise, such as sucking fingers or turning the head and opening the mouth. The [NHS advises offering a feed at these early cues](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/the-first-few-days/), before crying makes feeding harder. Stop when your baby needs a feed, even if you have only recently left home.

Our [guide to newborn feeding cues](/breastfeeding-tracker/blog/newborn-feeding-cues/) explains what to notice. A feed before leaving does not give you a fixed window in which your baby will not need another one, so leave room for stops instead of planning around a set gap.

Once you have a seat, put the bag down and get comfortable. Keep your shoulders relaxed and bring your baby close to you. Their head and body should be in line, with their neck, shoulders and back supported. The [NHS positioning and attachment guide](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/positioning-and-attachment/) explains these checks and how to bring your baby to the breast without leaning forward over them.

**Take your baby out of a sling or carrier before feeding.** The NHS says feeding while they remain in a sling or carrier is unsafe. Keep their nose clear and their face visible; check that your top has not fallen across it.

# Do I have to use a feeding room?

A dedicated room is an option if you prefer it. You do not need to find one before every outing, and the [NHS is clear that you should not feel you have to breastfeed in a public toilet](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/breastfeeding-in-public/).

In England, Scotland and Wales, the Equality Act protects against less favourable treatment because you are breastfeeding when receiving services. See the [government's explanation of breastfeeding protection](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/equality-act-guidance/individuals-a-summary-guide-to-your-rights-html) and its [guidance on the Act's geographical scope](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/equality-act-2010-guidance).

If someone interrupts, a short reply such as “I'm feeding my baby” may be all you want to say. A companion could speak to staff for you. You can also move if that is what you prefer; you do not have to settle a disagreement while feeding.

# If feeding is difficult away from home

If the seat is awkward, ask for a different chair or move somewhere you can support your baby comfortably. If you want help with positioning, ask a midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding supporter to watch a feed. Explain what is difficult, for example, “I can feed with cushions at home, but I struggle to support the baby in a cafe chair.”

For feeding questions, the [National Breastfeeding Helpline, listed by the NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/baby/feeding-your-baby/breastfeeding/breastfeeding-in-public/), is **0300 100 0212**, open 24 hours a day. If you have pain or concerns about how your baby is feeding, ask a midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding supporter for help. The [NHS advice on sore nipples](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding-problems/sore-nipples/) recommends getting help early; changing the venue alone will not assess feeding difficulties.
