# It wasn’t a clean switch from one way of feeding to another

When breastfeeding became painful, we did not wake up one morning and decide that we were now a combi-feeding family.

There was no carefully planned transition.

We were tired, my wife was in pain and our daughter still needed feeding. We had already decided not to have her tongue tie divided, so we needed to find a way forward that worked around the reality in front of us.

At first, introducing a bottle felt like a much bigger decision than it probably looked from the outside. We had planned to breastfeed exclusively. Giving expressed milk or formula felt like moving away from that plan, even though breastfeeding was still part of almost every day.

What helped was realising that feeding did not have to fit into one neat category.

Breastfeeding could continue.

Expressing could give us another option.

Formula could be there when we needed it.

That combination became combi feeding for us.

# Expressing gave us some breathing room

The biggest change was not simply that our daughter could take milk from a bottle. It was that feeding no longer depended entirely on one exhausted person.

When my wife expressed milk, I could give a bottle while she rested. I could take responsibility for a feed rather than standing nearby, trying to help around the edges. In the middle of a difficult few weeks, that mattered to both of us.

Expressing also helped us stop thinking about breastfeeding and bottle feeding as opposites. A bottle did not automatically mean the end of breast milk, and expressing did not mean every feed had to follow the same pattern.

Some feeds were directly from the breast, often with nipple shields because they made feeding far more comfortable. Some were bottles of expressed milk. Sometimes we used formula.

It was less tidy than the plan we had made before our daughter arrived, but it was much more sustainable for the family we had become.

# Learning to express took time

Expressing can sound straightforward when it is reduced to a single sentence: collect milk now so your baby can have it later.

In reality, it is another skill to learn while caring for a newborn.

The [NHS guidance on expressing breast milk](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/expressing-breast-milk/) explains that milk can be expressed by hand or with a pump, and that the right approach depends on why you are expressing. It also notes that it can take a little while for milk to begin flowing. Feeling relaxed, having your baby or a photograph nearby, gentle massage and warmth may help.

That was a useful reminder for us: expressing was not a test that had to be passed immediately. A difficult session did not mean it would always be difficult, and the amount collected in one session did not define whether the whole approach was working.

If you use an electric pump, the NHS advises building the suction up slowly. Expressing should not bruise or catch the nipple, and different funnel sizes may be available if the fit is uncomfortable. Pumps, bottles and collection containers also need to be cleaned and sterilised as directed.

Those practical details are worth getting right, but comfort matters too. If expressing is painful or difficult, the NHS recommends asking a midwife, health visitor or breastfeeding supporter for help rather than simply enduring it.

# Storing milk without adding more mental load

Once we began expressing, there was suddenly more to remember.

Which milk had been expressed first?

What needed to go in the fridge?

What had already been warmed?

The NHS recommends using sterilised containers or breast milk storage bags, storing milk in small quantities to reduce waste, and labelling and dating milk before freezing it. It also advises defrosting frozen milk in the fridge when possible, never refreezing milk that has been defrosted, and never using a microwave because it can create hot spots.

Storage times depend on the temperature and where the milk is kept, so check the current [NHS expressing and storage guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/breastfeeding/expressing-breast-milk/) rather than relying on a number remembered from an old note or late-night search.

We found that the goal was not to build a perfect freezer supply. It was simply to make the next feed a little easier when we could.

# Combi feeding let breastfeeding continue

The phrase “combi feeding” can mean different things to different families. The NHS uses “mixed” or “combination feeding” for combining breastfeeding with bottles of expressed breast milk, infant formula, or both.

Its [guidance on combining breast and bottle feeding](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/combine-breast-and-bottle/) explains that changes may take time. It also notes that introducing formula can affect milk supply and recommends making changes gradually where possible, both to give the body time to adjust and to reduce the chance of uncomfortable, swollen breasts or mastitis.

For us, combi feeding was not a single routine that we followed perfectly. It was a set of options.

If breastfeeding felt comfortable, we breastfed.

If my wife needed rest and expressed milk was available, I gave a bottle.

If we needed formula, we used it.

That flexibility reduced some of the pressure we had placed on every feed. Breastfeeding did not disappear. In fact, having other options helped it remain part of our daughter’s feeding without asking my wife to push through pain and exhaustion every time.

# Sharing feeds changed more than the practical workload

Giving bottles allowed me to do something concrete.

I could settle into a chair with our daughter, learn her cues and be the person she looked to for that feed. My wife could sleep, shower or spend time with our eldest without being the only person who could answer the next hungry cry.

It did not make the early weeks easy. Expressing itself takes time and energy, bottles create washing and sterilising, and deciding what kind of feed comes next can become another piece of mental admin.

But it made feeding feel more shared.

That was the part we had not understood before we needed it.

# Finding the version of feeding that works for your family

Moving to combi feeding can bring relief and guilt at the same time.

We worried that changing the plan meant we had given something up. Looking back, it meant the opposite. We found a way for breastfeeding to continue while making room for rest, recovery, expressed milk, formula and another parent who wanted to help.

Every family’s reasons will be different. You may be expressing because your baby cannot latch well, because you are returning to work, because someone else will be helping with feeds, or simply because you need more flexibility. You may use expressed milk, formula or both.

You do not have to justify that decision to make it valid.

If you are worried about feeding, milk supply, pain or your baby’s wellbeing, speak to your midwife, health visitor, GP or a breastfeeding supporter. A blog can share one family’s experience, but it cannot tell you what is right for you and your baby.

For us, combi feeding was not the feeding journey we had pictured.

It was the one that helped all four of us move forward.
